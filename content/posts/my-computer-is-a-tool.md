I thoroughly enjoy homelabbing. Most tech people probably prefer playing games on their computer, and I do as well, but I prefer managing my containers and my servers.

## A Lot Changed Lately

There have been some big changes in my life. I was a NASA contractor, but recently I was picked up by NASA. That is a great thing, but it has come with a lot of distractions. When a person's job and well-being change suddenly, their thoughts can drift away from other important things in life.

Needless to say, it has been a challenge, but it has been worth it. And I noticed something along the way: sitting down at my server pulled my focus into that task and away from the work stuff.

As I write this, I ask myself: why would I rather look at Docker Compose YAML files and Markdown notes than play a game? Why is the terminal more appealing to me than a game when I sit down at my computer?

## Two Kinds of Reward

One way I think about it is the difference between external and self-generated rewards. Dopamine itself isn't neatly split into "types" like this, so take it as a lens, not a lab result. It's how it *feels* to me.

Games are built around externally delivered rewards: loot drops, killing an enemy, leveling up. If you enjoy the game, you keep playing and keep that cycle of challenge and reward going until you beat it. That reward is curated and predictable.

Others, like me, seem to prefer a "slow-burn" reward. There is nothing flashy on the screen, often not even a graphical interface. But when that Compose file runs without syntax errors and your application launches, some of us get a quiet, self-generated payoff from overcoming friction and mastering a system. For my brain, that hard-earned feeling beats the one a game hands me.

In psychology, *Need for Cognition* (NFC) is a personality variable reflecting how much a person is inclined toward effortful thinking. People low in NFC tend to find mental exertion draining and want entertainment that lets them power down. People high in NFC, like me, tend to enjoy complex thinking, analysis, and organization. Organizing knowledge in Markdown, troubleshooting a syntax error, or working out a configuration file isn't draining for me. It's energizing. Games often feel too rigid to satisfy that craving.

## Whose Rules Are We Playing By?

No matter how open-world a game is, you are still inside a closed system, playing by the developer's rules instead of your own.

A terminal gives you complete agency, with no limits beyond your own skill and logic. It's an infinite sandbox. You are the locus of control. You own the system, and consuming pre-packaged media can't compete with that.

## The IKEA Effect

In 2011, researchers Michael Norton, Daniel Mochon, and Dan Ariely published a study called "The IKEA Effect: When Labor Leads to Love." They found that people place a much higher value on things they build themselves. In one experiment, people who assembled an IKEA piece valued it more than people who were handed the same piece already assembled.

A couple of examples fit this idea. The story goes that in the 1950s, boxed cake makers removed the powdered egg from their mixes and had people add fresh eggs instead, which supposedly eased the guilt of buying a prepackaged cake and boosted sales. (The details of that story get debated, but the idea is a fun one.) Maybe people just feel better about something when they put a little work into it.

Another example comes from a company that I, and many others, are huge supporters of: LEGO. It has been loved by all ages for close to a hundred years. Why do LEGO sets sell so well? Maybe because the joy of building the toy is just as significant as the toy itself.

## My Computer Is a Creative Tool

I built my computer to handle any game you could throw at it, but I would rather play games on my Steam Deck and use my computer to create something. My computer is very much a creative tool rather than a toy, although I do use it for sim racing.

I follow a couple of YouTubers who get up early, before the family wakes up, to grab some uninterrupted gaming time. I do the same thing, but I'd rather be working on a config file or spinning up a new container for an application I plan to use.

## Taking It to the Extreme

I take it to the extreme in some cases. I have several Docker containers built into a single Compose file. Alongside the main container, I have another that notifies me through ntfy when a container update is available, and a separate one that lets me monitor the hardware and network resources each container is using. In some cases there's yet another that lets me reach these services from anywhere in the world, as long as I have a network connection.

What's the best part? I built it! I didn't write the code for these applications at a granular level, but I put this giant technological puzzle together on my old computer, and I would much rather spend my time on it than playing a game.

## Why It Matters Right Now

Looking back, it makes sense that the server became my anchor during all the change at work. Everything else was shifting, but in my homelab, every outcome comes down to my own effort and logic. When life gets unpredictable, a system I can understand, fix, and improve is a comforting place to spend an early morning.

I have nothing against those who like to play games on their computer. This is just my personal preference.
